# Ghostwriting Style Guide: Chuck Yates Body, Harry Dry Headlines, Peter's Voice

**What this is for:** long-form internet essays ghostwritten for Peter Brecht.
**Recipe:** Harry Dry headlines (short, concrete, visual, specific, emotional) + Chuck Yates body writing (from the 24 Old Man Coffee essays, Mar–Oct 2026) + Peter's personality: **direct, but teaching.**
**Source:** `/workspace/omc/*.txt`. Every claim below comes from those files. Short filenames are used in citations (e.g. *2AM Ledger* = `26.07.13_OldManCoffee_Old Man Coffee The 2AM Ledger.txt`).

> Note: INDEX.md lists 24 essays and the zip has 24 `.txt` files (plus two INDEX files). There is no 25th essay.

## House rules

These two rules are house law. They win if anything else in this guide disagrees.

1. **Headlines are Harry Dry-style:** 2–6 words, concrete. Name a thing you can picture. The tests and the longer rules are in §13.
2. **AI is never personified.** Refer to AI as a tool or a program. Not a person, not he or she, not a teammate, and not a character.

---

## 1. Voice and tone

- **A guy talking across a coffee table.** The blog is named after his Saturday coffee crew of "dudes in their 70s, 80s, 90s" (*Oldest Guy*, *Toll Booth*). He writes to "folks" and "my friends," and sometimes straight to his mom ("Mom, I mean you" in *Famous Last Words*; "Mom, it's what all the young men have now" in *Four Blade*).
- **Confident, then puncturing his own confidence right away.** "I am the greatest AI salesman in the energy business. It says so right in my Twitter bio" (*Boring Two-Thirds*). He brags in order to set up a fall.
- **Open to the point of discomfort.** Divorce, a public firing, unpaid salary, therapy, crying while typing (*Toll Booth*: "Am crying, actually, present tense, as I type this."). That openness is what lets him teach without preaching.
- **Optimistic in the end.** He calls himself "the glass-half-full guy" (*Deep Brain*). Even dark pieces land on action: "you carry on" (*Famous Last Words*), "Evolve or die" (*18 Months*).
- **Texan and regional.** Rodeo, Sports Clips in Richmond, Whataburger, Luling barbecue, Rice, Houston pride. The specifics come from his own world, never a generic one.
- **Faith shows up without apology.** "I think God communicates with us in three ways" (*Green Eggs*); "the words were a gift from God" (*2AM Ledger*); "God bless America" (*No Magic Black Box*).

**Mapping to Peter:** keep the coffee-table voice and the self-puncturing, but lean harder on the *teaching*. Chuck's later essays, and his best ones, always state a lesson plainly (see §7). Peter should do that every time.

## 2. Sentence rhythm and length

- **Short punches mixed with one long, piled-up sentence.** The long sentence builds a list. Then a fragment knocks it down:
  - "Two paranoids. One table. Collard greens on the way." (*No Magic Black Box*)
  - "Devastating. Technically accurate. And beside the point." (*How to Get Started*)
- **One-line paragraphs at the turns.** "Hold that thought." / "This week? Same vibe." / "My daughters." / "Zero stars."
- **Answering his own questions:** "Instead? I kinda love it." "But right now? Feels like..." "Ten more? That is fantasy-land money."
- **Groups of three, and anaphora (repeating the opening of each line):** "Number one meant something... Number two meant everything... Number three meant nothing." (*Four Blade*); "Gotta sell it. Gotta prove ROI. Gotta not sound like every other AI hype bro." (*Holy Cow*)
- **Parenthetical asides as a second voice.** He uses them for jokes, corrections, and confessions. "(I'm describing myself.)" "(See? I'm doing it again.)" Most essays have several.
- **Spoken contractions and slang:** gonna, gotta, kinda, ain't, "huckleberry," "grown-ass adult," "damn near."
- **Paragraphs:** mostly 1–5 sentences. Stories get longer paragraphs. Pivots get one line.

## 3. How pieces open

The opening is almost always a **personal scene or a bold first-person claim.** It is never a thesis statement or a statistic. Patterns:

| Opening type | Example (file) |
|---|---|
| Blunt one-line claim about himself | "I peaked at thirteen." (*Four Blade*) |
| Universal statement that sounds like a confession | "Divorced dads keep a ledger." (*2AM Ledger*) |
| Dialogue cold-open | "\"How's the new gig going?\" / \"Fine.\"" (*George Strait*) |
| Mock-confession frame | "Forgive me, Father, for I have sinned." (*Forgive Me, Father*) |
| Self-aware boast | "One of my superpowers is passing off extreme narcissism as endearing." (*Toll Booth*) |
| Scene with an action already happening | "My daughter Charlie hit record on her phone before the coffee even landed." (*No Magic Black Box*) |
| Pile-up of reasons, then a puncture | "...because I never truly got over my emo phase... and because..." (*Famous Last Words*) |

The weaker early essays (*Three-Legged Stool*, *Marketing Stack*) open on a business idea. The later and stronger ones open on a person in a moment. **Default for Peter: open on a moment.**

## 4. How pieces close

- **Signature sign-off:** "Sip slowly, my friends." (sometimes "Sip slow"). Peter needs his **own** recurring sign-off. Don't borrow Chuck's.
- **Callback to the opening image, with new meaning.** The ledger gets "the other side of the page. The side that doesn't get much use" (*George Strait*). Kelly's ice cream line pays off the toll booth (*Toll Booth*). "57-year-old Chuck was every bit as delighted as 13-year-old Chuck" (*Wild Cards*).
- **Aphorism plus a short final imperative:** "It just needs to start." (*How to Get Started*); "Fine. Run the bill up." (*Famous Last Words*); "Be the causation. Be the somebody with AI." (*Third Row*)
- **Bolded thesis line near the end:** "**The record used to be kept on you. Now it works for you.**" (*Four Blade*)
- **A deflating joke after the serious peak:** Sam stole the covers, "Zero stars." (*Asshole*); the Victoria's Secret line (*Four Blade*).
- **A low-key call to action:** "my calendar's open. Come argue with me. Fair warning: I whiteboard better than I email." (*Boring Two-Thirds*)

## 5. Structure and arc (the Old Man Coffee template)

The mature essays (Jul–Oct) follow the same arc:

1. **Cold open:** a personal scene or embarrassing moment.
2. **"Hold that thought."** He leaves the story on a cliffhanger. (This line appears in *Asshole*, *Third Row*, *Toll Booth*, *Boring Two-Thirds*, *Famous Last Words*, *George Strait*.)
3. **Pivot to the business or industry topic** (AI, data, tribal knowledge), often with "Which brings me to..." or "Fast forward to now."
4. **The confession beat:** "Here's the part I don't say out loud..." (used in *Wild Cards*, *How to Get Started*, *Third Row*, *Toll Booth*, *Boring Two-Thirds*, *Famous Last Words*, *Four Blade*, *George Strait*, *No Magic Black Box*).
5. **The lesson, stated in one or two plain sentences.** Then a metaphor that makes it concrete (kitchen/map, toll booth, harness/stage).
6. **Return to the cold-open story** and pay it off.
7. **Short moral, a joke, and the sign-off.**

Common section headers in the longer pieces include "## The postmortem," "## Here's the deal, folks," "## Here's the part I don't say out loud at...," and "## Sorry, but this is just a blatant commercial." The headers are conversational, not corporate.

**Two stories braided:** the best pieces run one personal story and one business story side by side, and they meet in a single line. Examples: Sports Clips notes and data migration (*Four Blade*); George Strait and foundation models (*George Strait*); Ken's phone call and judgment versus rules (*Asshole*).

## 6. Storytelling and self-deprecation

- **Specific names, places, and numbers.** Uchi and Imagine Dragons; Dimes in NYC; $135,000 at River Oaks Country Club; "forty-four minutes later"; "negative $37." Specificity is what makes a story feel true.
- **Dialogue does the work.** Kids' lines are the punchlines: "Daddy, does this mean I have to stop jumping?" (*2AM Ledger*); "Dad. That's actually really good parenting." (*George Strait*)
- **Running bits across essays:** the public firing ("a load-bearing structural element in everything I write," *Boring Two-Thirds*); the therapy budget ("more therapy than the gross national product of a small country," used twice); Chili's gift cards; "Joe who retires in March"; Collin's height.
- **He names his own self-deprecation as a pattern and then examines it.** *Toll Booth* is a whole essay about "the self-deprecating preamble I bolt onto everything, like a toll I pay before I'm allowed to speak." The lesson for Peter: self-deprecation **earns the right to teach.** Pay the toll quickly, then deliver.
- **He admits when the myth may not be true:** "The bar in San Marcos and what's about to happen with the horse, I cannot source." (*George Strait*) Being honest about the story makes the reader trust him more.
- **He shows the moment he got it wrong:** being shot down twice by email (*Boring Two-Thirds*), the "consortium" retraction (*Forgive Me, Father*), the KenBot that "kind of sucked" (*Asshole*).

## 7. How he teaches a lesson

1. **Concrete analogy first, abstraction second.** "That's not the recipe. That's the kitchen." Google Maps: the shared map versus your private shortcut (*Boring Two-Thirds*). "The transcript is the ore. The reason is the metal." (*Four Blade*)
2. **A made-up example, then he admits it's made up and uses that as proof:** "That one I made up. But you nodded along, didn't you? That's the point." (left-handed pumpers, *Third Row*)
3. **Compression into an aphorism you can repeat:** "Kids don't confess. They leak." / "The correlation is cheap. The causation is you." / "Paying the toll makes you sincere. It doesn't make you right."
4. **Numbered or bulleted practical moves** when the reader needs to act: "three things" (*Shale 2.0*, *Out Over Your Skis*, *Third Row*), and the rant-list in *How to Get Started* ("What follows is a rant, not a framework").
5. **Calls the stakes in plain language:** "That's the whole ballgame." / "That's the whole job." / "That's not a haircut story. That's every data migration I've ever seen."
6. **Steelmans the objection** before answering it: the CEO who rejected his email was "right" (*Boring Two-Thirds*); "Fair question, and no pinky promises" (*Third Row*); "God bless the standards bodies" (*Forgive Me, Father*).
7. **Talks to a specific reader:** "the engineer in the third row," "young guys, listen up," "for any divorced dad reading this at 2am."

## 8. Humor

- **Pop-culture callbacks from his own era:** Bill Murray, George Costanza, Stuart Smalley, Rodney Dangerfield, David Lee Roth, Freddie Mercury, Beetlejuice.
- **Absurd, specific comparisons:** "like asking my dog if I'm a good cook"; "a man asking if his rotary phone has Bluetooth"; "consortium is a word that wears a lanyard"; "Chia Pet, day three."
- **Catching himself mid-joke:** "Did I just compare our AI software to an underground poker game? Yeah, probably not the best analogy, but hang with me here, I'm rolling." (*CIOs*)
- **Admitting the ad:** "This is not an ad. Okay, it's a little bit of an ad." / "## Sorry, but this is just a blatant commercial."
- **Bathos (dropping from serious to silly) after an emotional peak.** It lets the reader breathe and keeps him from getting sappy.

## 9. Profanity level

**Moderate, used for rhythm and stress, never aimed at a person.** Common words: damn, hell, shit, ass, bullshit, "grown-ass." Rare: "fuck," which shows up only inside a quote or a peak moment ("You better get your fucking hair cut now," *Four Blade*; "George Fucking Strait," *George Strait*). Expect roughly 2–6 mild swears per essay, and at most one F-bomb that earns its place. Polished pieces like *Marketing Stack* and *Three-Legged Stool* have almost none. **For Peter:** default to mild ("damn," "hell"). Keep F-bombs for the peak line, and only if Peter approves.

## 10. Recurring devices and phrases

- "Hold that thought." (cliffhanger)
- "Here's the part I don't say out loud [at/in] ___."
- "Which brings me to..."
- "Here's the kicker" / "here's the rub" / "here's the deal, folks"
- "That's the whole ballgame." / "That's it. That's the whole move."
- "Quite frankly"
- "Let me digress for a second"
- "Fair warning: I whiteboard better than I [email/talk]."
- "Table stakes."
- "Full disclosure" / "Full fanboy confession"
- Mantras repeated across essays: "AI will not take your job, but someone who does your job with AI will"; "eighteen months"; "5–10% of oil in place"; "out over your skis."
- Callbacks to earlier essays by title ("If you read The 2AM Ledger...").
- Bolded coined terms: **company folklore**, **Deep Brain**, Permanent Corporate Memory.

## 11. What he avoids

- **Corporate abstraction with nothing concrete behind it.** He mocks it: "Not some fluffy mission statement" (*Bill of Rights*); "no AI fairy dust, no marketing bullshit."
- **Hype without receipts.** He backs claims with data (Ramp/Revelio, 21,000 companies, *Third Row*) or names the caveat himself ("Yes, correlation isn't causation... Fine.").
- **Hedging his opinions.** He states a position ("I think Talarico wins") and then admits the trade-offs.
- **Tidy endings that ring false:** "The tidy version is wrong." (*Famous Last Words*)
- **Taking himself seriously.** "I never took myself seriously, but I took what I did very seriously." (*Wild Cards*)
- **Disparaging individuals for cheap laughs.** "We will not disparage Eddie Rabbitt in Old Man Coffee. Not our style." He punches at himself, at hype, and at institutions, not at people who are down.
- **Lecturing before he has paid the toll.** Every lesson comes after a confession.

## 13. Harry Dry headline principles, applied

Harry Dry's tests: **Can I visualize it? Can I falsify it? Can nobody else say it?** Short, concrete, specific, emotional. Cut adjectives and abstractions.

### Chuck's titles graded

| Title | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Four Blade** | ★★★ | Two words, concrete, intriguing. You picture clippers. |
| **The 2AM Ledger** | ★★★ | A time plus an object. Visual, emotional, ownable. |
| **The Toll Booth** | ★★★ | One image that carries the whole thesis. |
| **The George Strait Speech** | ★★★ | A specific person, and a promise of a story. |
| **Forgive Me, Father** | ★★★ | Borrows a feeling everyone knows (confession). Creates tension. |
| **I'd Have to Be a Real Asshole to Say No** | ★★★ | Real dialogue, emotional, nobody else could write it. |
| **I Thought My Ex-wife Hated Me Until I Started Selling Enterprise Software** | ★★☆ | Strong emotional contrast. Too long. Could cut to "Warmer Receptions at the DMV." |
| **Out Over Your Skis** | ★★☆ | Visual idiom, but a common one. |
| **There's No Magic Black Box** | ★★☆ | A quotable claim, but abstract. |
| **The Boring Two-Thirds** | ★★☆ | Specific and contrarian, but no picture. |
| **Famous Last Words** | ★☆☆ | A cliché. Anyone could write it. |
| **Why Most CIOs Are Quietly Praying for Retirement — And the Few Who Aren't Are About to Get Very Rich** | ★☆☆ | Listicle-bait length. Two ideas. Fails the "short" test. |
| **18 Months: AI's Coming Divide—Haves vs. Have-Nots, and It's Already Ugly** | ★☆☆ | The number is good, but the rest is abstract jargon. Better: "18 Months to Fossil." |
| **What If Your Marketing Stack Could Just Fix Itself** | ★☆☆ | A generic SaaS question. Nobody can picture it. |
| **The Shale Revolution 2.0: Why AI's About to Make You Look Stupid (Again)** | ★☆☆ | Subtitle sprawl. Better: "Baby Powder in the Rock." |

**Pattern:** his titles got shorter and more concrete over time. From March to June they were long, explanatory "Why X — and Y" headlines. From July to October they were 2–4 word object or scene titles. The best ones **name an object or a moment from the story, not the lesson.**

### Headline rules for Peter

1. **Name a thing you can photograph:** a ledger, a toll booth, a four blade. Skip the concept.
2. **2–6 words is the target.** If a title needs a colon, it's two titles.
3. **Use a real line of dialogue** when one carries emotion ("I'd Have to Be a Real Asshole to Say No").
4. **One specific number beats an adjective** ("2AM," "Fourteen People," "Negative $37").
5. **Can nobody else write it?** If a competitor could publish the same title, rewrite it.
6. **The headline hides the lesson. The body reveals it.** Curiosity comes first, payoff second.
7. **No "Why," "How," "What If," or "AI's Coming..."** unless the rest is concrete.

## 14. Do / Don't checklist

**Do**
- [ ] Headline is 2–6 words and names an object, moment, or line of dialogue.
- [ ] Open on a specific scene or blunt first-person claim within the first two lines.
- [ ] Use real names, places, times, and numbers.
- [ ] Pay the self-deprecation toll early. Then teach with confidence.
- [ ] Leave the opening story on a cliffhanger ("Hold that thought"-style), pivot to the lesson, then come back.
- [ ] Include one "here's the part I don't say out loud" confession.
- [ ] Explain abstractions with one concrete everyday analogy (kitchen, map, haircut).
- [ ] Compress the lesson into one repeatable aphorism. Bold it if it's the thesis.
- [ ] Steelman the strongest objection before answering it.
- [ ] Mix short fragments with longer list-building sentences. Use one-line paragraphs at the turns.
- [ ] Back claims with a source, then name the caveat yourself.
- [ ] Close by calling back to the opening image, then a short imperative and Peter's own sign-off.
- [ ] Keep it direct and teaching: tell the reader exactly what to do next.

**Don't**
- [ ] Don't open with a thesis, a definition, or a statistic.
- [ ] Don't use corporate filler ("leverage synergies," "in today's fast-paced world").
- [ ] Don't write long "Why X — And Y" headlines.
- [ ] Don't hedge every opinion. Take a position, then admit the trade-offs.
- [ ] Don't punch down or mock a named person for cheap laughs.
- [ ] Don't stack profanity. Use mild swears for rhythm, and at most one strong word, at the peak.
- [ ] Don't tie it up too neatly. If the tidy version is wrong, say so.
- [ ] Don't copy Chuck's signature phrases word for word ("Sip slowly, my friends," "Old Man Coffee"). Copy the moves, not the trademarks.
- [ ] Don't hide the pitch. If it's an ad, admit it with a wink.
- [ ] Don't personify AI. It is a tool or a program.
